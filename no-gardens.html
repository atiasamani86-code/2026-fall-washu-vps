There Are No Gardens on the Internet

My Media Response — Chia Amisola

When I first opened this website, I wasn't sure what I was supposed to do. It didn't feel like a normal website. I had to learn how to move around, find a key, and enter a garden. I liked this because it made me curious about what I might discover next.

One thing that really caught my attention was seeing poetry by Omar Khayyam, one of my favorite Iranian poets. His poetry was translated into English, but I recognized his name. Khayyam often questions what we really know about life, time, and existence. He writes about how life is short, how uncertain the future is, and why the present moment matters. I thought it was interesting to find his poetry in a digital garden. It made me think about how we spend our time online and whether we actually pay attention to what we see.

While exploring the garden, I also ended up on another website with an article called "The Garden and the Stream." I wasn't expecting that. I thought I was exploring one artwork, but suddenly I was reading something written in 2015. It made me think about how the internet connects different people, ideas, and moments in history.

Another part I liked was a quote from Ross Gay about spending time in a garden and learning to pay attention. Usually, when I visit a website, I want to find something quickly. But here, I had to slow down and explore.

This experience also reminded me of my own project, The Food Archive. I'm building a website inspired by traditional Iranian cabinets. Each cabinet opens to a different period in food history. After experiencing this artwork, I started thinking about how I could make people more curious about what is behind each door.

I think my biggest takeaway is that a website doesn't always have to give us everything immediately. Sometimes discovering something is just as important as finding it.
